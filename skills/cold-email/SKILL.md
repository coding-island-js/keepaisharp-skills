# Write a cold email that gets a reply

The person getting this email does not know you. They will give it a few seconds on their phone. This skill
writes an email that gives them a real reason to read it (something they said or did), shows you can help
(one true line), and asks for one small thing they can say yes to. Nothing is guessed. The user sees it
before it goes.

## Step 1. Find one checked fact about them

Look for something the person or their company said or did themselves, recently:

- Their own post (LinkedIn, X, a blog), a talk or interview they gave.
- A filing, a press release, a news story about them, a job opening they posted.
- Their website, if it says something specific (a new location, a new product, a stated problem).

Open the source and read it yourself. Copy their exact words if you will quote them. Write down the link and
the date. If you cannot find a fact you can check, say so to the user. Do not make one up, and do not use
fake flattery like "I was impressed by your profile."

## Step 2. Say what that fact usually means

One sentence: what someone in their spot usually has to deal with next. Keep it concrete. "Two sets of
logins and two email systems" is concrete. "Digital transformation challenges" is not.

## Step 3. Write one proof line that is true

One sentence on who you are and one real result you or your team got on something similar. A number, a
customer type, a time frame. It must be something the user can explain and back up if asked. If the user
has no proof that fits, say so and ask them for one. Never claim revenue, clients or results that do not
exist.

## Step 4. Ask for one easy thing

One small ask they can answer with "yes": a 15-minute call, a free one-page plan, a sample you already made.
Do not ask them to explain their plans or their problems ("Where do your leads come from?",
"Tell me about your goals"). That is homework for a stranger, and it stalls the reply.

## Step 5. Put it together, 50 to 125 words

In this order:

1. **Subject.** Two to four words, their topic in their words ("New location phones"), not yours.
2. **The fact.** One sentence, with their exact words in quotes where it matters.
3. **What it usually means.** One sentence.
4. **Proof.** One or two sentences.
5. **The ask.** One short question.
6. **Your name** and how to reach you.

Count the words. Under 50 usually skips the proof. Over 125 usually hides the ask.

## Step 6. Read it as the buyer

Read the email again as that person, on a phone, between meetings. Answer honestly:

- Do I know in five seconds who this is and why they wrote to me?
- Is the fact about me right? Does it match the source word for word?
- Could I say yes to the ask without thinking hard?
- Does anything sound like a template or a sales pitch?

Fix whatever fails, then read it as the buyer one more time.

## Step 7. Cut the soft words

Delete or rewrite: "just", "I think", "I hope", "maybe", "kind of", "sort of", "a bit", "sounds like",
"hopefully", "either way", "if that makes sense", "I feel like". State the thing, then stop.

## Step 8. Show the user before sending

- Show the subject and email as a draft. Never send it without the user's OK.
- Under the draft, list the source link and date for the fact, and where the proof line comes from.
- Mark anything you could not confirm **UNCONFIRMED**.

## Example

The user types: "Write a cold email to Dana Cole, operations manager at Brightline Plumbing. I set up phone
answering for small service companies." (Dana, Brightline and the numbers below are made up.)

The agent finds Dana's LinkedIn post from last week: "Third location opens Monday. Now we figure out how to
answer three phones with one office." The agent saves the link and date, and asks the user for one real
result. The user says: "Set up call answering for a two-shop HVAC company last spring. Missed calls went from
about 30 a week to under 5."

The draft:

> Subject: Third location phones
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> Saw your post: "Third location opens Monday. Now we figure out how to answer three phones with one office."
>
> That usually means calls ringing out at the busy times, and jobs going to whoever picks up first.
>
> I set up call answering for small service companies. Last spring a two-shop HVAC company went from about
> 30 missed calls a week to under 5.
>
> Want a one-page plan for your three locations? It's free.
>
> Alex Kim
> alex@example.com

That is 83 words. Under the draft, the agent lists the post's link and date, and notes that the HVAC result
comes from the user.
